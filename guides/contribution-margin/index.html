Contribution margin explained, with worked examples

Contribution margin is the part of each sale left over after variable costs. It is the money that "contributes" first to covering fixed costs and then to operating profit. Almost every break-even question starts here.

The three forms of contribution margin

Per unit: contribution margin = selling price − variable cost per unit
Total: total contribution margin = total sales − total variable costs
Ratio: contribution margin ratio = contribution margin ÷ sales (per unit or in total)

OpenStax Managerial Accounting §3.1 defines all three. They describe the same thing at different scales: per unit for "what does one more sale add?", total for "what did this period's volume add?", and the ratio for "what does each dollar of sales add?"

Worked example (illustrative)

A product sells for $40. Materials cost $18 per unit, packaging $2, per-unit shipping $3, and a 5% sales commission adds $2. Variable cost per unit is therefore $25.

  • Contribution margin per unit = $40 − $25 = $15
  • Contribution margin ratio = $15 ÷ $40 = 37.5%; the variable cost ratio is the remaining 62.5%
  • At 500 units, total contribution margin = 500 × $15 = $7,500

If monthly fixed costs are $5,000, operating income is $7,500 − $5,000 = $2,500. Laid out in the contribution format:

Contribution-format income statementAmount
Sales (500 × $40)$20,000
Less variable costs (500 × $25)($12,500)
Contribution margin$7,500
Less fixed costs($5,000)
Operating income$2,500

The contribution format groups costs by behavior (variable vs fixed), while the traditional format used for external reporting groups them by function (cost of goods sold vs operating expenses). The contribution format is what makes volume questions easy to answer.

Contribution margin vs gross margin

ItemContribution marginGross margin
SubtractsAll variable costs (production and selling)Cost of goods sold
Fixed production overheadExcluded (treated as fixed cost)Often included in cost of goods sold
Variable selling costs (commissions, per-order shipping)IncludedUsually excluded (below gross profit)
Main useInternal volume, break-even and product decisionsExternal reporting and pricing comparisons

In the example, if cost of goods sold were $18 of materials plus $4 of allocated fixed factory overhead, gross profit per unit would be $18, higher than the $15 contribution margin, because commissions, packaging and shipping are not in cost of goods sold. Neither number is "right"; they answer different questions. Gross margin and markup are covered on the sibling site ameti.capital.

Three decisions contribution margin helps with

1. Break-even and target volume

Divide fixed costs by contribution per unit to get break-even units ($5,000 ÷ $15 ≈ 333.33), or by the ratio to get break-even revenue ($5,000 ÷ 0.375 ≈ $13,333.33). The units vs revenue guide goes deeper.

2. A one-off order at a lower price

Suppose a buyer offers $32 per unit for 100 extra units, you have spare capacity and fixed costs will not change. Contribution per unit on the order is $32 − $25 = $7, so the order adds 100 × $7 = $700 to operating income. If the order required an extra $1,000 of fixed cost (say, a temporary hire), it would reduce income by $300. Contribution margin makes that comparison explicit; it does not decide whether the lower price affects your other customers.

3. Comparing products

A $60 product with $30 of variable cost has a higher contribution per unit ($30) and a higher ratio (50%) than the $40 product (37.5%). When a shared resource such as machine hours is the bottleneck, compare contribution per unit of that scarce resource rather than per unit sold.

Common mistakes checklist

  • Leaving variable selling costs (commissions, card fees, per-order shipping) out of variable cost.
  • Treating allocated fixed overhead as if it were variable, which understates contribution margin.
  • Mixing periods: monthly fixed costs with annual volume.
  • Using list price when most sales happen at a discount.
  • Reading a high contribution ratio as high profit; profit still depends on volume and fixed costs.
