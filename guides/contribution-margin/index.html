Contribution margin primer

Contribution margin is the amount left from a sale after variable cost. That remainder is what can help cover fixed costs — and, after that, whatever else you are measuring. Educational definitions only.

Two related numbers

Contribution margin per unit = price − variable cost per unit
Contribution margin ratio = contribution margin per unit ÷ price

With a $40 price and $25 variable cost, contribution is $15 per unit and the ratio is 15 ÷ 40 = 37.5%. Multiply the per-unit contribution by volume to estimate total contribution for a period — still subject to your fixed/variable labels.

Why the label “contribution” matters

People often say “margin” for several different percentages. Contribution margin specifically asks: after costs that move with the unit, how much is left toward the fixed block? Gross margin language on a P&L may use a different cost scope. Always check what sits in the cost line before comparing percentages across tools or statements.

Link to break-even

When contribution per unit is positive, a simple break-even model divides fixed costs by that contribution:

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per unit
Break-even revenue = break-even units × price

Try the numbers in the break-even calculator. If contribution is zero or negative, that model does not produce a useful break-even unit count — each additional unit does not help cover fixed costs under the labels you chose.

Worked comparison

Two products can share a 40% “margin” label and still behave differently at break-even if one figure is contribution on price and another is gross profit on a different cost base. Write down the denominator and the cost list. Illustrative only: Product A at $100 price / $60 variable → $40 contribution (40% ratio). Product B at $100 price / $70 variable → $30 contribution (30% ratio). The same $6,000 fixed block needs 150 units of A or 200 units of B in this toy model.
